Pop-culture lesson · DTC e-commerce
Friends — The One with the Cop (S5E16), Ross's 'PIVOT!'

Every DTC brand has a "PIVOT!" moment in Q4.

You know the scene. Ross, Rachel and Chandler are trying to get a couch up a staircase. Ross has a plan: yell "PIVOT!" louder each time.

The couch ends up cut in half.

That's what BFCM looks like for most support teams:
→ Order volume triples overnight
→ Everyone jumps into the inbox
→ Nobody agreed on who owns returns, refunds or WISMO
→ The founder's plan is to answer faster

Yelling "PIVOT!" at your helpdesk isn't a plan.

We've scaled a DTC support operation from 1 person to a 6-person 24/7 team. The couch made it up the stairs because of three boring things we set up before the rush:

1. SOPs for the 5 ticket types that make up ~80% of volume
2. Macros written in the brand's voice, not "Dear Valued Customer"
3. A handover doc between shifts so nothing gets left on the landing

Black Friday is about 8 weeks away.

What's your couch this year? The thing you know will get stuck on the stairs?

Operator insight · Logistics & delivery
No pop-culture hook: straight operator content

In logistics support, the customer who contacts you 3 times usually had one problem the whole time.

Here's why. In delivery support, the first contact is rarely the actual problem. It's the symptom:
"Where's my package?" usually means one of these:
• The scan hasn't updated in 36 hours
• It was delivered to the wrong unit
• The driver marked "attempted" and never came back

Each one needs a different fix. When agents only have a tracking link, they send the tracking link. Then the customer calls again.

We run a delivery support team where each person handles 90–100 calls a day. That volume only works because every call type has its own path:
→ Dispatch inquiries go to live dispatch visibility, not a canned reply
→ "Delivered but missing" gets an investigation workflow with a set callback time
→ Repeat contacts get flagged so the third call goes to a senior agent

The team started with 3 dedicated people. It's now 6.

If your repeat-contact rate is high, the problem usually isn't how fast you answer. It's that the answers don't solve anything.

Most support teams are full of Jesses. Talented, well-meaning people, all improvising:

Every account we take on starts the same way before a single ticket gets answered: Discover → Design → Deploy → Operate → Optimize. Workflows, macros and escalation paths get written down, so the 3rd agent we add gives the same answer as the 1st.

Say my name? No thanks.
Say the SOP version number instead.

Pop-culture lesson / case study · Hardware & consumer tech (applies to DTC too)
Friends: 'The One Where No One's Ready' (S3E2), Joey wearing all of Chandler's clothes: 'Could I BE wearing any more clothes?'

Could we BE on any more channels?

Joey said it best, doing his Chandler impression while wearing every piece of Chandler's clothing at once. Piling everything on doesn't make you better prepared. It just makes it harder to move.

A lot of brands launch support the same way. Email, live chat, phone, WhatsApp, Instagram DMs, all on day one, with the same two people trying to cover them all.

The result is slow email, a chat widget nobody's watching, and a phone line that goes to voicemail.

For a 3D-printer brand we support, we did the opposite:
→ Email first. Macros, SOPs and a tagging system until response times were steady.
→ Chat next, once email quality held. Live troubleshooting needs agents who already know the product inside out.
→ Phone last, with a clear L1 → L2 path, because a frustrated caller with a failed print needs an answer, not a hold queue.

The team grew from 4 to 14 people, and support now runs 24/7 across all three channels.

Add a channel when you can run it well, not just because the tool lets you switch it on.

Which channel did you open too early?

Pop-culture lesson · Robotics & consumer tech
The Office: Dwight Schrute's 'Question… False.' format

Question: What's the #1 reason a $3,000 robot gets returned?

"It's defective."
False.

In the robotics queues we have worked, a lot of returns start before the sale. The customer bought the wrong model for their yard, their floor plan or their use case, and nobody asked the right questions before checkout.

That's why we think pre-sales is part of support. It isn't only sales.

For one consumer robotics brand we started with a 3-person team doing only pre-sales:
→ Pre-bookings and purchase guidance
→ Product-fit questions ("Will this handle a 30° slope?")
→ Setting honest expectations before the order

The 3 questions we ask before every robot sale:
1. Where will it actually run? Yard size, slope, floor type, obstacles.
2. What does success look like in week one?
3. Who's setting it up, and when? (Saturday morning is the usual answer.)

Three questions before checkout save a return, a refund and a 1-star review after it. That pre-sales team later grew into a full 24/7 pre- and after-sales operation.

Bears. Beets. Better pre-sales.

Robotics folks: what's the question you wish every customer asked before buying?

Pop-culture lesson · DTC e-commerce
Seinfeld: 'The Soup Nazi' (S7E6), 'No soup for you!'

A lot of DTC return policies read like the Soup Nazi wrote them.

"Returns accepted within 14 days, unworn, tags attached, original packaging, receipt required, final sale items excluded, exchanges only, customer pays shipping…"

NO REFUND FOR YOU. 🍲

Here's what I've learned from running DTC inboxes where each agent handles 100+ emails a day: customers aren't upset about the policy itself. They're upset that they didn't understand it until they needed it.

A strict policy is fine. A surprise policy isn't.

What actually lowers return-related tickets:
→ Put the policy in plain words on the product page, not only in the footer
→ Proactive "how to exchange for a different size" emails after delivery
→ Macros that explain why, not just what ("final sale because it's a sample item")
→ Agents who can offer an exchange before the customer asks for a refund

The Soup Nazi had lines out the door because the soup was incredible.

Your product probably isn't that good. (Sorry.) Make returns easy to understand.

Pop-culture lesson / framework · Logistics & delivery
Breaking Bad: Mike Ehrmantraut's 'No more half measures' (S3E12)

"No more half measures." — Mike Ehrmantraut

The most common half measure we see in logistics support: hiring more agents without building an escalation path.

It looks like progress. Headcount goes up and the queue drops for two weeks. Then it's back, because every hard case still ends up with the one person who knows how dispatch actually works.

Full measures look like this:

1. Define L1 vs L2. Write down what the frontline resolves and what gets escalated, and make it a document, not a gut call.
2. Give each escalation a destination. "Escalate to ops" isn't a destination. A named queue with a response-time target is.
3. Close the loop. L2 fixes go back into L1 macros so the same case doesn't escalate twice.
4. Grow L2 on purpose. In one operation we move experienced agents into L2 every quarter.
5. Measure repeat contacts, not just first reply time.

Half measures scale headcount. Full measures scale the operation.

Which step do most teams skip, in your experience?

"Yes, Chef."

If you've watched The Bear, you know the chaos in that kitchen isn't about talent. It starts when nobody knows who owns which station.

Once the brigade system clicks, the same people who were panicking during service start running it smoothly.

A support team works the same way.

🔪 Stations = channels. Email, chat and phone each have owners, not "whoever's free."
🧾 Tickets on the rail = queues with clear priority. The next thing to do is never a mystery.
🗣️ "Heard" = acknowledgment. Customers get told "we've got this" before the fix lands.
👨‍🍳 Sous chef = your L2. Somebody senior is always on the line, not in a meeting.
🧽 Close-down = shift handover notes. The next shift inherits a clean kitchen.

"Every second counts" is on the wall at The Bear. In support it's "every handover counts." Most dropped tickets I've seen were dropped between two people, not by one.

Pop-culture lesson · Logistics, DTC
Spider-Man: the 'Spider-Men pointing at each other' meme (with Brand New Day in theaters)

A package goes missing. Who owns the ticket?

🕷️ The brand points at the 3PL.
🕷️ The 3PL points at the carrier.
🕷️ The carrier points at the brand.

Meanwhile the customer is on hold. They didn't sign up for a Spider-Verse crossover.

This is one of the most common failures we see where logistics and e-commerce overlap. Everyone has part of the answer and nobody has the whole ticket.

How to end the pointing:
→ One owner per ticket, always on the customer-facing side, even when the fix sits with the carrier
→ Pre-agreed SLAs with your 3PL for investigations ("48h to confirm or reship")
→ A "lost in transit" macro that promises what happens next and when, not "we're looking into it"
→ Reship or refund rules the agent can apply without chasing a manager for approval

The customer shouldn't need to know your supply chain exists. They should just get their stuff.

Which link in the chain is hardest to get answers from: 3PL, carrier or internal ops?

Pop-culture lesson · All three — logistics, robotics, DTC
Friends: 'We were on a break!' (S3E15)

"WE WERE ON A BREAK!"

Ross and Rachel spent ten seasons arguing about it because they never agreed on what "a break" meant.

Support teams have the same fight every day, at shift change.

Day shift: "I told the customer we'd call back."
Night shift: "You told them WHAT?"
Customer: "It's been 14 hours."

Nobody did anything wrong. The context just didn't make it through the handover.

When you run 24/7 across time zones, the handover is the product. Here's the 5-line handover note our teams write at the end of every shift:

1. Promised: what we told customers we'd do, and by when
2. Waiting on: tickets blocked by another team or partner
3. Escalated: open L2 cases and who owns them
4. Weird: anything unusual (a spike, a bug, an angry VIP)
5. Changed: new SOPs, macros or product info this shift

It takes 5 minutes, and it prevents a lot of "we were on a break" moments.
